Lecture 1The T in CBT

What therapy is, and where CBT fits

The first class explained what modern therapy is, where CBT sits among other approaches, and what makes any therapy work. It introduced the triangle that the rest of the course builds on.

Lecture 2The C in CBT · Part 1

Noticing thoughts, and testing them

Automatic thoughts run constantly, aren’t chosen, can contradict each other, and are often distorted. A distorted thought is like a fun-house mirror: based on something real, but stretched. This class showed how much these thoughts shape how we feel, and how to test them.

Common thinking distortions

Catastrophizing / fortune telling

“I just know I’m not going to get this job.”Being sure the future will go badly.

Mind reading

“She didn’t wave back. She must be mad at me.”Being sure what others think.

All-or-nothing thinking

“If I get a B, my law-school experience is ruined.”Black-and-white, absolute terms.

Unfair comparisons

“I’ll never be as charismatic as that comedian.”Comparing yourself on one domain only.

Emotional reasoning

“I feel like I’m going to fail, so I will.”Watch for “I feel like…” followed by a whole sentence.

They overlap

“If I have another panic attack, I’ll have a breakdown.”All-or-nothing and fortune telling at once.
Lecture 3The C in CBT · Part 2

Updating thoughts and testing predictions

This class covered when to use a thought record, how the way we explain events affects us, how to come up with a better thought, and how to test fearful predictions in real life.

Questions for finding a better thought

  • What would you tell a friend in this situation?
  • Have there been times when this wasn’t true?
  • Are you ignoring any of your strengths?
  • What are the worst, best and most realistic outcomes?
  • If the worst happened, could you cope?
  • Is there another explanation?
  • Are you blaming yourself for something outside your control?

Say “and” instead of “but”

“You’re competent, but you struggle with some things.”

“You’re competent and you struggle with some things.”

“And” lets two true things stand together, so you don’t have to pick one. It’s also a core rule of improv and is useful at work. In the live role play, a new thought (“Life is a marathon, with ups and downs”) felt true in theory but not yet in the gut. That’s normal, and practice closes the gap.

Lecture 4The C in CBT · Part 3

Core beliefs, and the balance of validation and change

Under the passing thoughts are deeper layers that are harder to notice and have more influence. This class showed how to find them and how to loosen them, and why change only works alongside acceptance.

Three ways to challenge a core belief

Look at the words

Define them. What does “unlovable” or “worthless” actually mean? What would count as concrete evidence?

Use extreme contrasts

Name someone who is truly a total failure. Even serial killers have partners. Is the comparison fair?

Weigh the evidence

Confirmation bias means we count the evidence that fits and dismiss the rest. Make yourself count both sides.

Lecture 5The B in CBT

Behavioral activation and exposure

These tools are taught last, but in real treatment they usually come first, because they bring relief fastest. The rule of thumb: for low mood, use behavioral activation; for anxiety, use exposure.

When emotion hits 9–10: calm down first

The opposite of fight-or-flight is the dive reflex, which calms the nervous system. You can trigger it through the senses (name one thing you see, hear, feel and smell) or with cold water on your face or hands. With others, keep it gentle: “Let’s all take a few seconds, maybe stretch or have a sip of water.”

Wrap-upThe toolkit

Five tools, and when to use each

Feeling down or depressed→Behavioral activation: schedule pleasure and mastery, starting tiny Anxious or avoiding something→Exposure: approach gradually and let anxiety rise and fall A thought feels exaggerated→Thought record: evidence for and against, then a balanced thought The same theme keeps coming back→Downward arrow to the core belief, then challenge it A scary prediction→Behavioral experiment: do it or poll people, then record what you learned Emotion at 9–10→Calm down first: use the senses or cold water Someone shares something hard→Validate first (“Given…, it makes sense…”), then ask open questions
